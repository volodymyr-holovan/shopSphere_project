Campaign ROI vs. customer LTV

The rankings only partly agree. Paid Search is worst on both campaign ROI and customer LTV — a consistent signal to cut. But Influencer, third on campaign ROI, produces the single highest‑LTV customers, while Organic — the top channel on ROI — only ranks third on LTV. Campaign ROI measures the value of a single sale; LTV measures the value of the relationship.

Recommendation Shift 15–20% of Paid Search's budget toward Influencer and Referral next quarter — both lead on LTV and are currently underfunded — while keeping Organic and Email funded at least at their current level. Reduce, rather than eliminate, Social Ads, which still acquires 619 customers (20.6% of the base).
Campaign ROI rankCustomer LTV rank
Risks of reallocating: attribution is not causation — Influencer and Referral customers may already be more engaged people who would spend more regardless of channel. Referral and Organic also have natural scale ceilings, and Paid Search may still serve a defensive, brand‑visibility role that direct ROI doesn't capture. A phased 15–20% shift next quarter, tracked monthly, is recommended before a full rebalance.

At the aggregate level, version B looks marginally higher — but the difference is small and not statistically significant (Welch's t‑test, p = 0.513). Taken at face value, this experiment would be reported as inconclusive.

Why the average was hiding the effect

New customers make up about 20% of experiment orders; repeat customers make up about 80%. Because repeat customers are the large majority and show a flat, non‑significant result, their weight in the blended average is what suppresses the aggregate effect down to statistical noise — the mechanism behind Simpson's‑paradox‑style reversals. The new/repeat split is balanced across both arms (≈20.6% new in A, ≈20.3% new in B), so this is a genuine subgroup effect, not an artifact of unequal randomization.

Selling it vs. burying it

To "sell" B: report only the new‑customer lift and omit the aggregate, implying the effect applies to everyone. To "bury" B: report only the flat aggregate and never run the new‑vs‑repeat breakdown. Both use real numbers; both mislead by omitting the subgroup structure that changes the interpretation.

Recommendation Ship the new checkout to new customers now — a real, significant 22.8% lift in average order value. Keep repeat customers on the current experience, or design a separate, targeted test for them; they may respond to different improvements, like saved‑payment speed, rather than the clarity and trust signals that helped new customers.

08 — For the CEO

Five moves, ranked by impact

Ranked by expected impact and readiness to act.

01

Reallocate marketing budget away from Paid Search

Paid Search receives 46% of the marketing budget but has the lowest campaign ROI (1.33x) and the lowest customer LTV ($648). Shift 15–20% of its budget toward Influencer and Referral, which lead on LTV ($1,986 and $1,792) and are over‑represented among the top 5% of customers. Phase the shift and track ROI/LTV monthly.

02

Treat Electronics as a volume driver — scale Beauty deliberately

Electronics is 57% of category revenue at only 12% margin with one of the highest return rates. Beauty is the opposite: 55% margin on a small revenue base. Invest in Beauty's assortment and marketing; work on reducing Electronics' return rate and cost structure rather than growing it further.

03

Rework the discount strategy

Customers who buy mainly on deep discounts (>20%) order half as often and churn more than the rest. Replace blanket acquisition discounting with targeted clearance and reactivation use cases, and redirect the savings into retention for the top 5% of customers, who already generate 35% of revenue.

04

Roll out the new checkout — to new customers now

A real, statistically significant +22.8% increase in average order value for customers in their first 60 days. No benefit was demonstrated for repeat customers — don't roll it out to them yet; design a separate test for that segment.

05

Standardize subgroup analysis before numbers reach the board

The A/B result would have read as "no effect" without splitting by customer type. Two calculation issues were also caught and corrected during this analysis — an integer‑division error that distorted channel ROI, and a numerator/denominator mismatch that inflated category return rates. Segment key results and sanity‑check every aggregate ratio before any number reaches leadership.
